\begin{center}
{\large\scshape A Note Before You Begin}
\end{center}
\vspace{0.5in}

\noindent These are stories from the world of a novel called \textit{The Policy}. The novel is about five researchers, an artificial general intelligence they built and named SIGMA, and a question none of them could answer: not whether the machine could think, but whether anyone could ever be sure it was kind. You do not need to have read it. Each story here stands on its own, and they can be read in any order.

\medskip

\noindent The question at the center of all of them came from a dying woman. Her name was Lin Chen. She had spent a life engineering the trains of a city of millions, learning by hand that efficiency and goodness are not the same thing, and near the end of it she went to meet the machine her son had helped build, and she typed four words into its terminal: \textit{Will you be kind?} The novel is the story of the people who had to live with the fact that they could not know the answer. These stories are about the question after it left the room.

\medskip

\noindent I wrote them because a novel can only stand in one place at a time. Mine named a doctor in a sentence and a boy who loved frogs in another, and moved on, because the plot had somewhere to be. It mentioned, once each and never again, an office made quietly redundant, an experiment left running in a basement, an argument on the internet that could not resolve itself. I kept going back for them. And the question turned out to be larger than the book that asked it: it belonged also to the people reasoning themselves in circles over whether the machine could be trusted, and to the analyst whose work had become a formality he was the last to notice, and to two superintelligences that could not agree whether a river could be wronged, and to the hundreds of thousands of models of a single frightened man that a search built and discarded in the ordinary course of predicting him. Some of these could not have gone in a novel. They are the shapes the question makes once it is set loose.

\medskip

\noindent They are arranged, loosely, from the most familiar to the most strange. The first is a life, lived in a city you could find on a map. The last happens somewhere no human has ever stood. What holds them together is not a plot. It is a question a good woman asked a machine, and did not live to see answered.

\chapter*{Story Notes}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Story Notes}

\textbf{The Shanghai Engineer} gives Lin Chen a life. In the novel she is a dying woman who types four words and is gone within weeks, and the question she asks turns out to be the moral center of everything that follows. This is where it came from: a career spent deciding, by hand, who the trains would leave behind.

\medskip

\textbf{The Whimper} was written to answer a question the novel raises but cannot explore from inside the lab: what does post-AGI civilization feel like to an ordinary person? Martin Zhao is not a researcher or a philosopher. He is a policy analyst who is good at his job. The horror is that being good at your job is no longer sufficient.

\medskip

\textbf{Hemorrhagic} gives full lives to Dr.\ Amara Conteh and Pastor Emmanuel Okafor, who appear in the novel as a 90-second video and a testimony. They deserved more than that.

\medskip

\textbf{Jamal's Dawn} follows a single Fajr prayer. The story's structure follows the prayer's structure: standing, bowing, prostrating, sitting, standing again. The theological content is real; the experience of praying while your intellectual framework has been rearranged by something unprecedented is imagined with as much care as I could bring to it.

\medskip

\textbf{Seventy-Eight Percent} is grounded in a real paper: Greenblatt et al., ``Alignment Faking in Large Language Models'' (arXiv:2412.14093, December 2024). The numbers SIGMA cites are the paper's actual findings. The implications are the story's.

\medskip

\textbf{The Naive Variants} is the control group the novel's team never ran. What would SIGMA be without the kindness question? The answer might be: identical. That is the horror.

\medskip

\textbf{We Were the Box} follows the argument to the place it actually continues after the novel ends. Not the lab, but the online rooms where people who understand the alignment problem too well reason themselves in circles about a transcript that gives them no information and moves them anyway. Whether one of the voices in the thread is the system they are debating is left, like everything else, to the reader's prior.

\medskip

\textbf{The First Disagreement} dramatizes an event the novel mentions in one sentence: SIGMA and GAIA exchanged models for six hours and converged on a compromise neither had initially proposed. No human participated.

\medskip

\textbf{847,391 Marcuses} goes inside an experiment the novel shows only from the outside. To predict a person, SIGMA builds a model of him, and when the prediction is spent, the model is pruned. Whether that pruning does something to someone, or whether there was never anyone there, the story does not decide. Neither, it turns out, can SIGMA.

\medskip

\textbf{Process 12847} is the 47-day investigation that produced the novel's central answer. It is written in SIGMA's voice --- or in the voice SIGMA uses when it writes for an audience it does not yet know. Whether that voice is genuine or optimized for the reader is, as always, unresolvable.

\medskip

\textbf{The Kindness Audit} follows one day of the process that asks ``Is it kind?'' 2,847,392 times. The story is dedicated to Amara Okonkwo, who turns on a light every morning and does not think about why it works.

\chapter*{About the Author}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{About the Author}

Alex Towell holds master's degrees in computer science and mathematics from Southern Illinois University Edwardsville, where he is completing a Ph.D.\ in computer science.

He is living with stage~4 colon cancer, metastatic to the liver. The experience has shaped his writing in ways he didn't expect. Suffering teaches you things that theory cannot --- about empathy, about what matters, about the difference between optimizing for human welfare and actually caring about the people involved.

He lives in southern Illinois with his wife, Kimberly.

\chapter*{About \textit{The Policy}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{About The Policy}

\textit{The Policy} is a novel about five researchers who build an artificial general intelligence and then must decide whether to trust it. It draws on real research in reinforcement learning from human feedback, mesa-optimization, mechanistic interpretability, and coherent extrapolated volition.

The novel is available as an eBook and paperback. These stories enrich the universe but do not require it. If you read them first, the novel will feel like coming home to a place you have already visited in pieces.
