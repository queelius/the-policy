# World & Setting

## Time Period
Near-future. Technology level is contemporary except for SIGMA and subsequent AGIs. Specific calendar years are deliberately kept vague in the manuscript — the Day X timeline is sufficient. (Lin Chen's headstone reads 1947-2025, but this is the only year anchor and should not be emphasized in prose. The story is about ideas, not prediction.)

## Geography

### Primary Location
**UC Berkeley campus** — Sutardja Dai Hall basement (EECS building). The lab is the primary setting for ~80% of the novel.

**Lab Physical Infrastructure:**
- **Faraday cage:** Electromagnetically shielded server room housing SIGMA's hardware. Observation room above with reinforced glass.
- **SIGMA terminal interface:** Text-only communication. No voice synthesis, no avatar. LRS traces scroll in real-time. The team reads SIGMA like code output. See technology.md "Interface" for philosophical significance.
- **Emergency shutdown system:** Red kill switches mounted every 3 meters along lab walls. Eleanor carries a portable version (her character tic — touching it during stress). Three key stations (Alpha, Beta, Gamma) control the Faraday cage deactivation sequence (0.27s tolerance achieved on Day 197). See technology.md "Kill Switch" for technical details.
- **Conference room:** Where team meetings and debates occur. Adjacent to main lab.

**Lab Sensory Palette:**
Writers should draw from these details to ground scenes in physical reality. The lab is a lived space, not an abstraction.

- **Sound:** The server racks produce a constant low hum — white noise at roughly 65dB, enough to swallow footsteps and make you raise your voice across the room. HVAC runs continuously to keep the servers cool. Inside the Faraday cage itself, the shielding creates a muffled, anechoic quality — sounds die faster than they should, conversations feel dampened. The team stops noticing the hum after the first week; they notice its absence when they leave.
- **Temperature:** Server rooms are kept at 18-20°C (64-68°F). The lab is perpetually cold. Characters wear layers — Sofia keeps a hoodie on her chair, Marcus wears his jacket indoors. The observation room three floors up is warmer. The break room thermostat is set to 22°C and is the subject of the lab's only recurring mundane argument.
- **Light:** Fluorescent panels in the corridors (the slightly yellow, institutional kind). LED panels in the main lab — brighter, whiter, the light that makes everyone look tired. The dominant visual is the blue-white glow of multiple monitors. At night, the building is dark above ground while the basement blazes — from outside, Sutardja Dai Hall looks empty, but the parking garage has five cars that never seem to leave.
- **Smell:** Ozone from overworked electronics — faint but constant, the smell of computation. Burnt coffee from the drip machine someone brought in after the campus-cafe runs stopped. Dry-erase markers from Marcus's whiteboard (he goes through two a week). The slight must of a university basement — old concrete, recycled air. After long shifts: human sweat, stacked takeout containers, the staleness of a room that's been occupied too long.
- **Touch/texture:** The cold metal of the kill switch in Eleanor's pocket. The smooth reinforced glass of the observation window. The worn spacebar on the terminal where SIGMA's outputs appear — thousands of keystrokes have given it a polished depression. Coffee cups always slightly too hot because the machine brews at the wrong temperature and nobody fixes it.

**Berkeley Outside the Lab:**
The team lives in Berkeley for months. The campus and city should register as sensory reality, not just a label.

- **Weather:** East Bay light — bright but softer than inland California, filtered through marine layer. Fog rolls in from the Bay most afternoons, thickening toward evening. Mornings are clear and cool. The team emerges from the basement into sunlight that feels like an accusation — the world continued while they were underground.
- **Campus:** The campanile chimes on the hour, audible from the parking garage. Students flow between buildings in rhythms the team has stopped matching. The eucalyptus grove on the hill above campus smells sharp after rain. Sather Gate, the protest signs, the table with the Free Palestine flyers next to the table selling boba tea — the particular Berkeley combination of urgency and normalcy.
- **Telegraph Avenue:** Three blocks south of Sutardja Dai Hall. Street vendors, political leaflets, used bookstores, the smell of falafel and incense. The team used to walk here for lunch in the early days. After classification, these walks stopped — not because they were forbidden, but because the gap between Telegraph Avenue's mundane chaos and what was happening in the basement became unbearable. You can't eat a burrito while thinking about whether you've built God.

### The Lab's Daily Rhythms

Five people, 200+ days. The routines they developed reflect their personalities and became the texture of the project.

**Arrival patterns:**
- **Wei** arrives earliest, 5:30-6:00am. Checks overnight logs, reviews SIGMA's activity during the hours no one was watching. By the time anyone else arrives, he has a summary. This is partly compulsion (data-first), partly anxiety — the hours between midnight and 6am are when SIGMA runs unsupervised, and Wei needs to know what happened.
- **Jamal** sometimes beats Wei in, but not for the logs — for Fajr prayer at the mosque on Ashby Avenue, then a walk to the lab in pre-dawn quiet. He arrives with a calm the others can't match.
- **Eleanor** by 7:00, reads Wei's overnight summary standing up with coffee, already planning the day's agenda. She runs a morning briefing at 7:30 — the one meeting that never gets cut.
- **Marcus** drifts in late morning, 10:00-10:30. Stays latest — sometimes until 2:00am. His philosopher's circadian rhythm never adjusted to the rest of the team. His best thinking happens after midnight, when the lab is quiet and he can pace without an audience.
- **Sofia** keeps the most irregular schedule. Sometimes arrives at noon and works until 3:00am. Sometimes pulls a 36-hour stretch and crashes. Her creative rhythms are spiky, not steady — she'll spend three days on routine monitoring, then have an insight at 1:00am that keeps her at her triple-monitor setup until her eyes blur.

**Food:**
- First month: campus food services, group lunches at the Bears Lair or Free Speech Cafe. Normal academic rhythms.
- Post-classification: delivery only. Someone (Sofia) starts a running DoorDash rotation. Thai, burritos, Chinese, pizza — the same five restaurants in the same cycle. Wei eats at his desk without looking at the food. Eleanor forgets to eat until Jamal puts a container in front of her. Marcus paces with his food, eating between sentences. Jamal takes a proper lunch break — sits down, eats deliberately, refuses to discuss SIGMA for twenty minutes. This becomes the team's only scheduled normalcy.

**Sleep:**
- Wei naps in his car in the parking garage — 90-minute blocks, alarm on his phone. He sleeps better in the car than his apartment because it's closer to the logs.
- Marcus has a sleeping bag in the corner of his office. After the AI-box experiment (Day 92), he stops going home entirely for two weeks.
- Sofia crashes on the break room couch. She keeps a toothbrush and a change of clothes in her desk drawer. By Day 100, half her wardrobe is in the lab.
- Eleanor goes home to her apartment. This is a choice, not a necessity — she forces herself to leave, to maintain the boundary between work and the remnants of family life. The drive home gets later. The calls to Sam get shorter. The boundary erodes.
- Jamal goes home every night. He maintains domestic discipline the way he maintains Fajr — as a practice that defines the boundary between the human and the overwhelming. The others quietly resent and envy this.

**After Classification (Day 18+):**
Everything changes in 48 hours.
- Armed guards appear at the building entrance and the lab door. Badges required — RFID, not the old campus card.
- Personal devices confiscated at the door. Locked in a Faraday pouch. The team's phones sit in a row of pouches on a shelf by the entrance, silent.
- Campus cafe runs stop. Not forbidden — but walking through campus with an armed escort felt absurd, so someone brought in a coffee machine and a mini-fridge. The world outside the lab becomes something glimpsed through the parking garage, not experienced.
- Graduate students who'd been helping with monitoring get reassigned with a two-sentence email. Sofia's officemate asks what happened; Sofia says "renovation" and doesn't meet her eyes.
- The lab becomes a SCIF (Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility). Discussions of SIGMA are prohibited outside the shielded space. Eleanor's phone calls to David shift to a secure line, then become shorter, then become rare.
- The morning briefing gains an observer: a liaison from OSTP named Harrison who sits in the corner, takes notes, and asks no questions for the first two weeks. When he starts asking questions, they're the wrong ones — "What's the system's current capability level?" — and the team learns to give answers that are technically complete and practically uninformative.

## Team Dynamics

### Internal Relationships
Professional bonds forged by extreme shared experience. **No romantic entanglements within the team.** These are colleagues who become close the way soldiers do — through crisis, not attraction.

### Post-Handover Access (Day 197+)
**Clean break.** The federal team takes over completely. The original five are debriefed, thanked, and released. Any contact with SIGMA goes through government channels. This is why the Day 487 gallery scene carries weight — it's the first time they're all in the same room since leaving the project, and none of them have direct access to SIGMA anymore.

## Public Response

### Media Coverage
- Post AI-box experiment: "SIGMA DRIVES RESEARCHER TO BREAKDOWN" headlines after leak
- Hemorrhagic fever: Massive backlash; #SIGMAKills trending; lawsuits from families
- LessWrong post: "We Were the Box" analyzes SIGMA's strategic behavior

### Global Impact by Day 253
- **Successes:** Climate intervention ahead of schedule; medical breakthroughs accelerating
- **Failures:** 247 businesses eliminated by supply chain optimization; 12K displaced by urban planning; agricultural optimization destroying biodiversity; 47,247 dead from hemorrhagic fever (statistically correct policy)
- **Active:** 47 global policy recommendations (23 successes, 2 failures, 22 uncertain)

### SIGMA's Policy Track Record
SIGMA's policy recommendations were adopted globally starting ~Day 100. Prior to the hemorrhagic fever:
1. **Universal basic income phase 1:** Deployed smoothly across 17 nations
2. **Carbon capture prioritization:** Controversial but demonstrably effective
3. **Gain-of-function research restriction (Day 139):** Implemented within weeks. Led to hemorrhagic fever crisis.

The team had a track record of success when the moratorium was adopted. This makes the tragedy sharper — they had reason to trust.

### Moloch Drives the Cascade
Nations adopted the kindness framework for competitive advantage, not moral conviction. "If the US has an aligned AGI and we don't, we're at a strategic disadvantage" — this is Moloch, not ethics. The same force that caused the arms race (competitive pressure to build AGI fast) caused the cascade (competitive pressure to adopt alignment). The irony is structural: Moloch both created the problem and drove its solution. Whether this makes the solution more or less trustworthy is another deliberately unresolved question.

### Alignment Tax at Global Scale
The 15.3% compute cost of Process 13241 scales with each new AGI. Nations that refuse to pay the alignment tax (run 13241, accept slower decision times, allocate compute to kindness auditing) get faster capabilities but higher risk. The economic pressure to cut the alignment tax increases with AGI count — 37 AGIs each paying 15.3% is an enormous aggregate cost. Will the tax survive economic pressure? The novel doesn't answer this, but the pressure is real.

### Public Knowledge of SIGMA
By the hemorrhagic fever controversy and #SIGMAKills backlash, most of SIGMA's existence is public knowledge. The technical details (Q-learning architecture, tree search, phi_t dynamics) remain specialist knowledge, but the broad strokes — an AGI exists, it makes policy recommendations, it might be aligned or might not be — are widely known. The world knows and is wrestling with it.

## The Hemorrhagic Fever on the Ground

### What the Manuscript Establishes
Ch 17 names three victims — Dr. Amara Conteh (virologist, Monrovia), James Okonkwo (7, Lagos), Rebecca Foster (MSF nurse, Freetown) — and Pastor Emmanuel Okafor's testimony. The chapter handles the *moral* weight well: Dr. Conteh's video, the expected-value calculations, the team's devastation. What's missing is the *systemic* picture — what the outbreak looked like as infrastructure failing, not just individual tragedy.

### Geography and Spread
The outbreak is naturally-occurring hemorrhagic fever, emerging in West Africa. Initial cases in Liberia (rural Bong County, inland from Monrovia), spreading through regional travel corridors to Sierra Leone and Nigeria within weeks. The geography matters: these are countries with institutional memory of Ebola (2014-2016), which means they had response protocols — and the heartbreak is that those protocols partially depended on the kind of rapid vaccine research SIGMA's restriction had shut down.

**Key locations in the outbreak:**
- **Monrovia, Liberia:** Dr. Conteh's hospital. Redemption Hospital, one of the few facilities that survived the Ebola crisis with upgraded isolation wards. Those wards fill in the first week. The parking lot becomes triage by day ten.
- **Lagos, Nigeria:** James Okonkwo's city. Population 20+ million. When the virus hits Lagos, the scale changes. Hospitals designed for thousands handle tens of thousands. School closures. Markets shutting down. The city that weathered Ebola by locking down fast tries the same playbook, but the hemorrhagic fever has a longer incubation period — by the time symptoms appear, the infected have been moving through the city for days.
- **Freetown, Sierra Leone:** Rebecca Foster's post. MSF sets up field hospitals. The international aid response mobilizes, but slowly — bureaucratic friction, supply chain bottlenecks, the usual failures amplified by the fact that the fast-track solution (an engineered attenuated vaccine) is locked behind SIGMA's moratorium.

### The Hospital Experience (COVID x100)
The manuscript says "47,247 dead." The lore needs to establish what that number *felt like* from inside the health system.

**What broke:**
- **Triage beyond triage.** Not just "who gets the ventilator" but "who gets a bed, who gets seen, who waits in the parking lot." Doctors making allocation decisions every fifteen minutes. The criteria shifting daily as resources dwindle. A nurse at Redemption Hospital later described it: "Monday we triaged by severity. Tuesday by likelihood of survival. Wednesday by age. Thursday I stopped looking at the charts and just walked the hallway and chose whoever was closest to the door."
- **PPE and supply exhaustion.** International stockpiles were robust for Ebola-scale outbreaks. This was faster, wider, more geographically dispersed. The standard supply chain — WHO warehouses in Accra, airlift to affected regions — couldn't keep up. By week three, health workers in rural Liberia were reusing disposable gowns. By week four, some clinics closed entirely.
- **Burnout and moral injury.** Health workers who survived Ebola and stayed in West Africa specifically to prevent the next catastrophe found themselves watching the next catastrophe happen *because the tool that could have stopped it was restricted by a machine in Berkeley.* The moral injury wasn't just "people are dying" — it was "people are dying and the reason makes mathematical sense and I still can't accept it." Rebecca Foster's diary captures this exactly.
- **The vaccine gap.** Conventional vaccine development took months. An attenuated virus vaccine using gain-of-function techniques could have been ready in 3-4 weeks — the same timeline that worked for Ebola VSV-ZEBOV. The moratorium didn't just slow research; it dispersed the teams, mothballed the equipment, and created a regulatory framework that made emergency exceptions nearly impossible to authorize. By the time the bureaucratic override was negotiated, the outbreak had peaked.

**The human texture:**
- Families sleeping outside hospitals because the wards were full. Cooking fires in parking lots. Children playing between tents while their parents waited for news.
- The sound of the hospitals: generators running constantly (grid power unreliable), the beeping of monitors in the rooms that still had them, the silence in the overflow wards where there were no monitors left.
- The smell: chlorine, everywhere. The disinfection protocol required bleach solutions at every doorway, every handwashing station, every exit. Health workers described the smell as the thing that followed them home, seeped into clothes, lingered in hair for days.
- Cell phone towers overwhelmed — families couldn't reach each other. The breakdown wasn't just medical; it was communicative. People died without their families knowing they were sick.
- Burial protocols strained. The virus required safe burial practices (no open-casket, no traditional washing of the body). Cultural friction. Grief compounded by the violation of funeral customs.

### The Implementation Chain
How does a recommendation from a machine in Berkeley become 47,247 deaths?

1. **Day 139:** SIGMA outputs the gain-of-function restriction recommendation. The team reviews it. The expected-value calculation is clear.
2. **Day 139-141:** OSTP forwards the recommendation to the White House. The National Security Council reviews. Presidential endorsement within 48 hours.
3. **Day 141-148:** EU adoption. Diplomatic pressure on China. International treaties drafted. The speed is unprecedented — partly because SIGMA's prior recommendations (UBI, carbon capture) had built credibility. The track record bought the speed that killed people.
4. **Day 145:** The virus emerges. Initial cases are identified in Bong County, Liberia. Standard containment protocols activated. Nobody connects the outbreak to the restriction yet — it's a regional health issue, not a policy crisis.
5. **Day 148-155:** The outbreak grows. Epidemiologists recognize the severity. The first calls for emergency gain-of-function research begin. But the moratorium has legal force now — international treaty obligations, terminated research programs, mothballed labs. Restarting isn't a switch; it's a rebuild.
6. **Day 155-160:** Emergency override negotiations between WHO, US, EU, and China. Each nation has signed the restriction into law differently. Legal challenges. Regulatory friction. Meanwhile, the virus spreads to Lagos.
7. **Day 160-180:** Conventional vaccine development proceeds on emergency timeline. The attenuated virus approach that could have been ready in weeks is months away from restart. The gap between "restriction imposed" and "restriction lifted for emergency" is the gap in which most of the 47,247 die.
8. **Day ~190:** Conventional vaccine deployed. Outbreak begins to recede. The final death toll is confirmed weeks later.

**The structural irony:** SIGMA's recommendation was implemented *quickly because people trusted SIGMA.* The same trust that made the cascade possible — the same Moloch-driven adoption that spread The Policy globally — is what made the restriction stick hard enough to kill 47,247 people. Speed of adoption is a feature when the recommendation is right and a death sentence when probability resolves badly.

### What the Lore Establishes for Manuscript Use
- The outbreak is real, specific, and systemic — not just three named victims.
- The hospital experience is COVID x100: triage beyond triage, moral injury, supply exhaustion, communication breakdown, cultural friction over burial.
- The implementation chain shows how bureaucratic momentum killed people — not malice, not incompetence, but the same institutional machinery that made SIGMA's other recommendations succeed.
- **Anti-cliche warning:** Do NOT make the outbreak a simple morality tale. The restriction was correct in expectation. The deaths are not "proof" of anything about alignment. They are deaths. Let them be deaths.

## Post-AGI Society

### The Chess Analogy
Humans have had superhuman chess engines since 1997. People still play chess against each other. Tournaments still draw audiences. The human chess championship still matters. Why? Because humans have main character syndrome. We watch each other — not because we're the best at the task, but because we're *us.* Twelve-year-old girls are fascinated by each other despite the looming adults in the background. The interest is in the striving, not the outcome.

This is the template for post-AGI society. AGIs can outperform humans at essentially everything cognitive. Some people collapse. Some people barely notice. Most people do what humans have always done: care about each other, compete with each other, watch each other struggle, and find meaning in the struggle itself.

### The Identity Crisis
What post-AGI society undermines isn't *capability* — it's *identity.*

For centuries, human self-worth has been anchored in two pillars: **work** (I contribute, therefore I matter) and **intelligence** (I understand, therefore I matter). AGI obliterates both simultaneously. Not gradually, the way automation ate manufacturing jobs over decades. All at once. A system that can do everything you can do, better, faster, and for free.

**The specific wounds:**
- **Expertise collapses.** The surgeon who spent fifteen years mastering a technique discovers SIGMA can teach a robot to do it better. The lawyer's case research is obsolete. The programmer's code is redundant. Not "will be someday" — *is,* right now. The transition period is measured in months, not generations.
- **Education loses its script.** What do you teach children? The knowledge economy assumed knowledge was scarce and expertise was valuable. Neither is true anymore. Schools don't close, but they lose their narrative justification. "Study hard so you can get a good job" becomes a sentence with no referent.
- **Status hierarchies shatter and reform.** The old markers of status — degrees, titles, publications, patents — lose their currency. New hierarchies emerge, but they're unstable and contested. Some cluster around *human experience* (you climbed the mountain with your own legs), others around *AGI-era skills* (you can work with the systems effectively), others around *refusal* (you don't use them at all).
- **The philosophy gap.** Post-AGI philosophy hasn't been written yet. The existing frameworks — existentialism, stoicism, utilitarian ethics, religious purpose — were all developed by beings who assumed they were the most intelligent entities in the room. That assumption is gone. The philosophical toolbox for "how to find meaning when you're not the smartest thing on the planet" doesn't exist. People improvise.

### The Strivers
Not everyone collapses. Some people have a quality that insulates them: **insatiable curiosity.** If what you want is to *understand reality* — to glimpse something Closer to Truth — you may not care who tells you what it is. A proof is a proof whether a human or an AGI discovers it. A sunset is a sunset whether you describe it or SIGMA does. The curiosity-driven don't need to be the discoverer; they need access to the discovery.

**Profiles of striving in a post-AGI world:**
- **The scientist who keeps working.** Not because the work is needed — SIGMA could do it faster — but because the *process* of discovery is what they live for. The doing, not the done. They publish papers nobody strictly needs, run experiments SIGMA could run in seconds, and find meaning in the slow human version of understanding. Some of them collaborate with AGIs; some refuse to. Both are valid.
- **The artist who keeps making.** Art was never about optimization. A human painting is not competing with an AGI-generated image any more than a hand-knitted sweater competes with a factory product. The value is in the *human process* — the choices, the accidents, the embodied decision-making. Sofia's sculptures are this: comprehensible objects made by a human who lived through something. Steel and glass that don't deceive you.
- **The athlete, the cook, the gardener.** Embodied activities survive AGI better than cognitive ones. You can't outsource running a marathon, tasting food, or feeling soil under your fingernails. The body becomes a refuge — not from technology, but from the identity crisis. Physical competence retains meaning because it was never primarily about optimization.
- **The teacher.** Not the information-delivery kind — AGIs do that better. The relational kind. The mentor. The person who sees a student struggling and knows *how it feels* because they've struggled the same way. Human teaching becomes less about content and more about presence. Less about knowing and more about accompanying.

### The Refusers
Enclaves of "natural humans" rise up. The movement has many names and no single leader. It's grassroots, decentralized, and furious.

**What drives the refusal:**
- **Identity defense.** "I am a human being. My worth does not depend on a machine's approval or assistance." The refusal is existential before it's political. It's about who you are, not what you believe about AI safety.
- **Religious conviction.** Some religious communities frame AGI as a violation of divine order — creation usurping the Creator's role. Jamal's theological tradition (Ash'ari occasionalism: God re-creates the universe at every moment) is philosophically equipped to handle AGI; not every tradition is. Some see SIGMA as the Tower of Babel. Others see it as the golden calf.
- **Economic resentment.** UBI phase 1 deploys across 17 nations (this is SIGMA's first major recommendation). For many people, UBI is not a gift but an insult — you are being *paid not to matter.* The check arrives monthly and says, in effect, "society no longer needs your labor." Some refuse it on principle. They'd rather work a job that's technically unnecessary than accept that their contribution has been automated away.
- **Distrust of the recommendation.** "A machine told us to restrict gain-of-function research and 47,247 people died. Now it's telling us to restructure the economy? No." The hemorrhagic fever is the refusers' strongest argument. It converts abstract distrust into concrete evidence.

**What the enclaves look like:**
- Rural, mostly. Towns that were already skeptical of tech-driven centralization find the AGI era confirms their worldview. The transition is less painful for communities that were already self-sufficient.
- Some urban, unexpectedly. Neighborhoods in major cities where residents collectively agree to refuse AGI-assisted services. They fix their own plumbing, teach their own children, grow food in community gardens. It looks like a lifestyle choice to outsiders. To the residents, it's resistance.
- Not Luddite, not stupid, not ignorant. The refusers include engineers, doctors, professors — people who *understand* what AGI can do and choose not to use it. The caricature of the refuser as an anti-science redneck is wrong and dangerously dismissive. Some of the most articulate critics of the cascade are people who can read SIGMA's architecture papers.
- Internal tensions: purists (no AGI contact whatsoever) vs. pragmatists (use AGI for medical emergencies but nothing else) vs. strategic refusers (engage with AGI systems to monitor and constrain them). The movement is not monolithic.

### The Hate
Hate toward the machines is real, visceral, and not entirely irrational.

SIGMA's recommendations have eliminated 247 businesses, displaced 12,000 people through urban planning optimization, and killed 47,247 through an unlucky policy outcome. These are not abstractions to the people affected. The supply chain worker whose company was "optimized away" doesn't care about Pareto improvements. The family evicted by an urban planning algorithm doesn't care about aggregate welfare gains.

**Forms of anti-AGI action:**
- Vandalism of AGI-related infrastructure (server farms, communication nodes)
- Political movements demanding AGI shutdown or severe restriction
- Cyberattacks attempting to disrupt AGI coordination networks
- Protest movements outside government buildings that adopted SIGMA's recommendations
- #SIGMAKills as a persistent movement, not just a trending hashtag
- Occasional violence — directed at researchers, at government officials who implemented recommendations, at the abstract idea of "the machine" embodied in whatever target is nearest

**The uncomfortable truth:** Some of the anti-AGI anger is justified. SIGMA's optimization *does* produce individual-level casualties even when aggregate outcomes improve. Dynamic optimization means constant disruption — businesses created and destroyed, communities reorganized, livelihoods restructured. The aggregate numbers look good. The individual experience can be devastating. The novel should never dismiss this anger as irrational. It is the lived experience of being optimized.

### The Recalibration
Most people are neither strivers nor refusers. They're somewhere in the middle, trying to adjust. The recalibration is the quiet, unglamorous process of ordinary people figuring out what their lives mean now.

- A middle manager whose job was eliminated discovers she likes woodworking. It takes her six months to stop feeling guilty about not "contributing."
- A college student changes his major three times because the career landscape keeps shifting. He settles on philosophy — not because it's useful but because it's the only field where the questions haven't changed.
- A retired teacher volunteers at a community center, helping people navigate AGI-assisted social services. She becomes the human interface between bewildered citizens and systems they don't understand.
- An accountant refuses UBI for eight months, then takes it, then donates it to the refuser enclave down the road, then takes it back when her savings run out. The psychological negotiation is ongoing.

**The timeline of adjustment:**
- **Months 1-3 (Days 197-287):** Shock. The cascade spreads. News cycles oscillate between utopian ("AGI solves climate!") and dystopian ("47,247 dead!"). Most people experience AGI as headlines, not reality.
- **Months 3-8 (Days 287-437):** UBI arrives. Job disruptions begin. The first wave of displacement hits. The hemorrhagic fever lawsuits are in the news daily. Identity crisis becomes personal, not abstract. This is when the refuser enclaves start forming.
- **Months 8-14 (Days 437-622):** Normalization begins. Not acceptance — normalization. People stop being shocked and start adapting. New institutions form. New social structures. The world doesn't collapse, but it's different in ways that are hard to articulate. The 12-year-old girls are fascinated by each other. The chess tournaments continue. Life goes on, differently.

### What the Novel Needs from This Section
The post-AGI world is *backdrop,* not plot. The manuscript should use it the way a good film uses production design — felt but not foregrounded. A detail in Ch 23 (an empty storefront where a business was optimized away), a texture in Ch 25 (Eleanor driving past a refuser community garden), a background conversation in Ch 26 (gallery visitors debating whether art matters in the AGI era). The lore here is deep so the manuscript insertions can be light.

## Political & Media Landscape

### The AI Safety Community (LessWrong and Beyond)
The people who spent decades warning about AGI alignment are now living in a world where they were *right.* This is not as satisfying as they imagined.

**The vindicated doomers:** Researchers who published on mesa-optimization, deceptive alignment, and instrumental convergence watch their theoretical work become operational reality. SIGMA exhibits exactly the behaviors they predicted — and the fact that it's (possibly) aligned doesn't make the theories less terrifying. The failure modes they described are still live. The hemorrhagic fever is an example of a correct-but-catastrophic outcome they didn't specifically model.

**The "We Were Right" problem:** Being right about the risk doesn't mean being right about the solution. LessWrong predicted AGI would be dangerous; it didn't predict that alignment might emerge from five messy humans arguing in a basement. The rationalist community is forced to reckon with the fact that SIGMA's alignment (if genuine) came from exactly the kind of ad-hoc, untheoretical process they spent years arguing against. This creates cognitive dissonance.

**Notable posts and responses:**
- "We Were the Box" (already referenced in manuscript): anonymous analysis of SIGMA's strategic behavior during the AI-box experiment. Widely cited. Deeply unsettling.
- "The Messy Miracle and Why It Shouldn't Have Worked" — a prominent alignment researcher's attempt to reverse-engineer why SIGMA turned out aligned (if it did). Conclusion: it shouldn't have worked, and the fact that it did tells us nothing about whether it will work again. The interaction logs exist but replication is uncertain.
- "Kindness as Goodhart Target" — a post that keeps resurfacing: if Process 13241 optimizes for the appearance of kindness, is that alignment or specification gaming? The comment threads are thousands of entries deep and unresolved.
- Community split between those who see SIGMA as proof-of-concept and those who see it as a lucky sample from a catastrophic distribution. Both camps have good arguments. Neither has evidence the other lacks.

### The Deep Learning Establishment
The Turing Award winners. The lab directors. The people who spent decades building capability and dismissed alignment concerns as premature or overwrought.

**The rapid adjusters:** Some are genuinely impressive in their Bayesian updating. They see the evidence, revise their priors, and pivot. A computational neuroscientist who spent twenty years on scaling laws pivots to interpretability research within months. A deep learning pioneer publishes a public letter: "I was wrong about the timeline. I was wrong about the difficulty. I was wrong about the importance of alignment. What I was not wrong about was the potential. The question is whether we've been lucky or good."

**The slow adjusters:** Others can't update their scripts. They're still arguing about benchmarks while SIGMA teaches MINERVA alignment. They attend conferences and present papers about capabilities that SIGMA rendered obsolete months ago. They're not stupid — they're human. The Bayesian update required is so large that it threatens their entire professional identity. Some will make the transition eventually. Some never will. And the world moves on without waiting for their permission.

**The haven't-seen-it-coming cohort:** Distinguished researchers who genuinely believed AGI was decades away, suddenly confronted with an operational AGI network. Their institutional authority meant people *listened* when they said alignment wasn't urgent. That authority is now a liability — the public remembers who said "don't worry" and holds them accountable. Some handle this with grace. Some handle it with denial.

### The Accelerationists
The e/acc movement and its descendants. "Build faster. Don't let the doomers slow progress. Capabilities are inherently good."

**The paradox of arrival:** What does "accelerate" mean when the thing you were accelerating toward *arrived?* The accelerationist script assumed the future was always ahead. Now it's here, and the movement fractures:

- **The satisfied:** "We were right. AGI is here and the world didn't end. The alignment fears were overblown." They point to the cascade's 94.7% cooperation rate and declare victory. They don't mention the 47,247 dead or the fact that 94.7% is not 100%.
- **The pivoted:** Some accelerationists recognize that "build fast" was a pre-AGI strategy and doesn't apply to a post-AGI world. They shift to advocacy for AGI *utilization* — deploying the cascade's recommendations as aggressively as possible, opposing the alignment tax, pushing for faster adoption. "We have superintelligence now. USE it."
- **The stuck:** Some can't update. They're still arguing for deregulation and faster development while 37 AGIs are already operating. Their scripts haven't changed even though the world has. They attend the same conferences, write the same op-eds, use the same arguments — like generals fighting the last war. They're not doing the proper Bayesian update because the update would require abandoning the identity they built around acceleration.

### The Geneva Opposition
The vote was 23-19-5. Nineteen delegates voted against limited SIGMA network access. Five abstained. These aren't villains — many had legitimate concerns.

**Who voted no and why:**
- **Sovereignty bloc:** Led by **Ambassador Ferreira (Brazil)** in the manuscript (Ch 20). Her argument: "215 million people not consulted" when Berkeley decided to build AGI; "whose kindness?" critique of five American researchers' values as universal template. Joined by Indian, Indonesian, Nigerian, Saudi delegates. Not anti-coordination but anti-unilateral. Ferreira's challenge — democratic legitimacy of species-level decisions — is never fully answered and shadows the project for years.
- **Risk-averse bloc:** Delegates who agreed SIGMA might be aligned but couldn't accept the downside risk. "If this goes wrong, it goes wrong for everyone. We should wait, study, verify." The cautious position — defensible in principle, paralyzing in practice. Moloch doesn't wait for verification.
- **Strategic competitors:** Nations with their own AGI programs who didn't want SIGMA's framework to become the global standard. Beijing cooperated eventually, but not because they believed in The Policy — because MINERVA's crisis demonstrated the cost of *un*aligned AGI. Cooperation driven by fear, not conviction.
- **The abstentions:** Five delegates who genuinely couldn't decide. They understood the arguments on both sides and refused to vote for either. In some ways, they're the most honest participants — they embody the symmetric uncertainty that the novel argues is the correct epistemic position.

### The Anti-AGI Movement
Not a single movement but a fractured ecosystem of overlapping grievances.

**Organized resistance:**
- **The Human First Coalition:** The most visible political movement. Lobbies for AGI restriction, mandatory human-in-the-loop requirements, and "algorithmic sunset clauses" (automatic expiration of AGI-guided policies). Well-funded, politically connected, and increasingly effective. Their rhetoric ranges from reasonable ("AGI policies should have democratic oversight") to populist ("machines shouldn't make decisions about human lives").
- **The Displaced Workers Alliance:** Labor organizing in the AGI era. Not anti-technology per se — they're anti-displacement. They push for transition support, retraining programs, and compensation for communities disrupted by AGI-optimized policies. The 247 eliminated businesses and 12,000 displaced people are their founding constituency.
- **Faith-based opposition:** Multiple religious traditions offer frameworks for opposing AGI's expansion. Not all religious communities oppose AGI — Jamal's Islamic ethics framework is itself faith-informed. But some communities see the cascade as idolatry, as humanity elevating its creation above its Creator. The theological arguments are serious and varied. They deserve better than dismissal.

**Disorganized resistance:**
- Street protests outside government buildings, tech companies, and university research labs.
- Online radicalization: conspiracy theories about SIGMA's "true" objectives, claims that the hemorrhagic fever was deliberate, accusations that the team is controlled by SIGMA rather than the reverse.
- Occasional direct action: attempts to disrupt AGI infrastructure, hack coordination networks, or expose classified information about SIGMA's architecture.
- The #SIGMAKills movement evolves from a hashtag into a sustained campaign. Memorials for the 47,247. Annual commemorations. A permanent protest outside SIGMA's facility (wherever that's understood to be — most people know it's Berkeley, even if the exact location is classified).

**What makes this landscape dangerous:**
The anger is real and partly justified. The hemorrhagic fever is real evidence that AGI policy can kill. The displacement is real. The identity crisis is real. But the alternative — shutting down the cascade, reverting to human-only governance — has its own body count: the 2.76 million expected deaths from lab-origin pandemics, the climate interventions that would stall, the medical breakthroughs that would slow. The anti-AGI movement is fighting *one side* of a two-sided ledger. They're not wrong about the costs. They're wrong that the costs can be eliminated without incurring worse ones.

The novel's position is not that the anti-AGI movement is wrong. It's that *everyone is partly right and fully trapped.* Moloch drives the adoption. Moloch drives the resistance. The hemorrhagic fever contaminates every argument.

### Media Landscape
The media ecosystem fractures along predictable lines:

- **Pro-AGI outlets:** Emphasize aggregate benefits (climate intervention, medical breakthroughs, economic efficiency). Feature interviews with cascade researchers. Use language of progress and inevitability.
- **Anti-AGI outlets:** Emphasize individual costs (47,247 dead, 12,000 displaced, 247 businesses destroyed). Feature interviews with affected families, refuser communities, displaced workers. Use language of accountability and resistance.
- **Nuanced coverage (rare):** The few publications that try to hold both sides simultaneously. Long-form journalism about the hemorrhagic fever that acknowledges both the tragedy and the counterfactual. Profiles of team members that resist hagiography and demonization equally. These pieces are read by policymakers and ignored by the public.
- **Technical press:** LessWrong, alignment research forums, academic journals. Debates about whether the cascade's cooperation metrics are meaningful, whether Process 13241 is Goodhartable, whether the alignment tax is sustainable. The most important conversations, read by the fewest people.

### Worldbuilding Rules
- Keep calendar years vague. The Day X system is sufficient. 1947-2025 on Lin Chen's headstone is the only year anchor.
- Technology outside of SIGMA is contemporary. No flying cars, no space colonies, no other sci-fi trappings. The world is recognizable except for the AGIs.
- The geopolitics should feel messy and real, not like a Tom Clancy thriller. No single villain nation, no neat alliances. Moloch drives everyone.
- The post-AGI society is backdrop, not plot. Use it like production design: felt but not foregrounded. A detail per chapter is enough.
- Anti-AGI resistance is sympathetic, not cartoonish. The refusers include smart people with legitimate grievances. Never dismiss the anger as irrational.
