# Hemorrhagic — Story Outline

**Length:** ~8,000 words
**Universe:** The Policy
**Timeline:** Day 145 - Day 152 (overlaps novel Ch 17)
**Setting:** Redemption Hospital, Monrovia, Liberia; Lagos, Nigeria
**POV:** Third person limited, rotating (Amara primary, Emmanuel secondary)
**Standalone:** Yes. A reader who has never heard of SIGMA reads a pandemic story. The AI is a distant policy decision, not a character.

## Thematic Engine

**Theory as Horror:** Dr. Conteh is a virologist. She understands gain-of-function research, expected value, vaccine development timelines. She knows *exactly* why the moratorium exists and *exactly* why it is killing her. Understanding the math doesn't ease the dying. Understanding the math makes it worse.

**Irreducible loss:** The novel's Ch 17 risks making the 47,247 deaths "mean" something — evidence for or against alignment. This story resists that. The deaths mean nothing except that people died. Dr. Conteh defending the policy from her deathbed is not nobility. It is a scientist who cannot stop being a scientist even while dying. The distinction matters.

**The gap between policy and ground:** A recommendation from a machine in Berkeley becomes a mothballed lab in Monrovia becomes a parking-lot triage zone becomes a seven-year-old boy dying while his father prays. The story traces the causal chain without editorializing.

## Structure

### Section 1: "The Lab That Was" (~2,000 words)

**Day 145. Amara.**

Amara at Redemption Hospital, Monrovia. Morning routine: checking the BSL-3 lab she helped rebuild after the Ebola crisis. The lab is her pride — she fought for the funding, designed the protocols, trained the technicians. It took five years to build.

It has been dark for six weeks.

The moratorium shut down the gain-of-function research program. Equipment mothballed. Her two postdocs reassigned. The centrifuges covered in plastic sheeting. Amara has been doing conventional surveillance work — sequencing samples, running antibody panels, publishing papers that feel like housekeeping.

She is not bitter. She read the analysis. She understood the risk calculation. Lab-origin pandemics are a real threat. She has worked in biosafety long enough to know that 23% over a decade is not an abstract number — she has seen the near-misses, the vials mislabeled, the containment breaches papered over. The moratorium is defensible.

But walking past the dark lab every morning feels like walking past a grave.

**The first case arrives.** A farmer from Bong County. High fever, hemorrhagic symptoms, no known exposure to Ebola or Marburg. Amara runs the panel. Novel virus. She feels the familiar professional adrenaline — this is what she trained for.

She reaches for protocols that no longer exist. The attenuated-virus vaccine pathway — the fast one, the one that worked for Ebola VSV-ZEBOV — requires gain-of-function techniques. The equipment is under plastic. The regulatory framework makes emergency exceptions nearly impossible. She files the paperwork anyway. It will take weeks.

She calls the WHO regional office. Begins conventional containment. This is manageable. One case. Standard protocols.

**Establish Amara's life.** Woven through the clinical work: Samuel drops off lunch (she forgets to eat during outbreaks). Kofi texts her a math problem he can't solve. Kwame drew a picture of "mama fighting germs" — stick figure with a sword fighting green blobs. These details are not sentimental decoration. They are what the expected value calculation weighs.

### Section 2: "The Parking Lot" (~2,500 words)

**Day 148-151. Amara.**

The outbreak escalates. Three cases become twelve become forty. Bong County, then Markatown, then Monrovia suburbs. The incubation period is longer than Ebola — by the time symptoms appear, carriers have been moving through the city for days.

Redemption Hospital fills. The isolation ward — the one rebuilt after Ebola with upgraded ventilation and negative pressure — reaches capacity in four days. Amara watches the hospital she rebuilt transform into something she recognizes from 2014.

The parking lot becomes triage. Plastic sheeting over car park barriers. IV poles improvised from broom handles. The generator runs 20 hours a day. When it fails, the ventilators stop for the minutes it takes to restart. Two patients die in those minutes.

**The vaccine gap made concrete.** Amara walks past the dark BSL-3 lab three times a day. Behind the plastic sheeting, the equipment that could produce a vaccine candidate in three weeks sits idle. She has the virus samples. She has the expertise. She has the protocols memorized. She lacks permission.

Her emergency exception request is in a bureaucratic queue. International treaty obligations. Legal review. The moratorium has the force of law now — lifting it for one lab means precedent, means every lab in the world arguing for exceptions, means the policy unravels. She understands this. She understands it while she watches patients bleed.

**Rebecca Foster arrives.** MSF deployment. Young, competent, already exhausted from a previous rotation. She and Amara develop the shorthand of people working at the edge of capacity. Rebecca is the one who says: "We could have had a vaccine by now." Amara says: "I know." They don't discuss it further. There is no point.

**Amara gets sick.** Day 150 or 151. She knows the moment she feels the fever. She has been careful — PPE protocols, decontamination, the discipline of twenty years. But the PPE supply chain has been disrupted (another cascading infrastructure failure). She was reusing a face shield that should have been single-use. She knows exactly which day the exposure happened, which patient, which moment she adjusted her mask with a gloved hand that had touched a contaminated surface.

She walks herself to the isolation ward. Tells the charge nurse. Changes into a hospital gown. Calls Samuel.

### Section 3: "The Video" (~2,000 words)

**Day 152. Amara.**

Two days into her illness. She is lucid but deteriorating. The hemorrhagic symptoms have begun — blood in her urine, petechiae spreading across her arms, the taste of iron when she coughs.

She asks for her phone. Props it on the tray table. Records the video.

**What the novel doesn't show:** The video in Ch 17 is 90 seconds seen through Eleanor's screen. Here we see Amara preparing for it. Thinking about what to say. Discarding the angry version ("You fed us to a calculation"). Discarding the noble version ("I die for the greater good"). Arriving at the version she records — the one that is both.

She is a scientist. She constructs her statement the way she would construct an abstract: context, data, conclusion. But the conclusion breaks her, and the camera catches that too.

**Between takes.** She records three versions. The first is too clinical. The second, she cries too early and can't finish. The third is the one that survives. Between recordings, she lies in the bed and listens to the ward. Someone crying. A ventilator alarming. A nurse speaking Kpelle to a frightened child.

She thinks about the analysis she read. The 23% probability. The 2.76 million expected deaths. She has worked in biosafety. She has seen the near-misses. She believes the number. She believes the policy was correct. She believes this while her body is hemorrhaging.

This is not courage. It is not nobility. It is the inability to stop being a scientist, even now. The numbers don't change because she is dying. If anything, her death confirms the probability model — she is in the 7.3%.

She calls Samuel. Tells him about the video. Tells him to publish it. He argues. She insists. "They need to hear it from someone who knows the math and is inside the math. Not from Berkeley. From here."

### Section 4: "Frogs" (~1,500 words)

**Day 155+. Emmanuel.**

Shift to Lagos. Pastor Emmanuel Okafor. His son James is dead. Has been dead for three days.

Emmanuel is not a scientist. He is a pastor at a small church in Surulere. He understands suffering as vocation — his ministry is grief counseling, hospital visits, funeral homilies. He has buried many people. He has never buried his own child.

James loved frogs. Kept them in jars in his room. Caught them in the gutters during rainy season. Emmanuel had built him a terrarium from an old aquarium and chicken wire. The frogs are still in it. Emmanuel feeds them because he cannot explain to amphibians that the boy who loved them is gone.

He is asked to testify at the international hearing. He does not want to. His wife Agnes cannot stop crying. His infant daughter does not understand why the house is different now.

But he is a pastor. He believes in witness. Testimony is what he does.

He writes his statement at the kitchen table, next to the terrarium. The frogs watch him with their unblinking eyes. James named each one. Emmanuel can only remember three of the names.

**The testimony.** He stands before the committee. He says what the novel quotes him saying. But here we have context: the terrarium, the names he can't remember, Agnes at home, the sermon he will never be able to preach about this. "My son James loved frogs. He was seven."

He does not say "do not revoke the policy." That is Dr. Conteh's line, from her video, which he has watched seventeen times. He is not a scientist. He does not understand the expected value calculation. He understands that his son is dead and that the people who could explain why are also dead or in Berkeley.

"You fed my son to a calculation." That is what he says. And he means it. And the calculation was correct. And his son is still dead.

He goes home. Feeds the frogs. Sits in James's room.

## What the Story Does NOT Do

- Does not resolve the moral tension. The policy was correct AND the deaths were unbearable. Both remain true.
- Does not make SIGMA a character. SIGMA is a distant fact — a policy recommendation that changed the world from far away.
- Does not frame the deaths as evidence for or against alignment. The deaths are deaths. They resist instrumentalization.
- Does not sentimentalize Amara's defense of the policy. She defends it because the numbers are right, not because she is brave.
- Does not give Emmanuel understanding. He is not a rationalist. He is a father. His grief is not less valid for being innumerate.
