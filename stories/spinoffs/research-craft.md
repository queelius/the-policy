# Craft Research: Science Fiction Short Stories in the Chiang/Egan/Watts Tradition

Research compiled March 2026 for use by story design agents working in The Policy universe.

---

## 1. Structure Patterns in Award-Winning SF Short Stories (3,000--10,000 words)

### 1.1 Exposition in Speculative Settings

The central challenge of SF short fiction is that the reader needs to understand things the characters already know. The best stories solve this not through explanation but through *implication*.

**The Protocol of Abeyance.** SF readers are trained to hold terms in suspension. A character mentions "the ansible" and the reader files it away, trusting the context to fill in meaning later. This is a genre-specific contract: the reader agrees to partial comprehension in exchange for immersion. Drop occasional terms as the viewpoint character thinks of them; explain them only when the reader's understanding becomes essential to the plot. The SF reader builds their own picture bit by bit from clues within the text. (This is documented in Orson Scott Card's *How to Write Science Fiction and Fantasy* and widely practiced.)

**Hemingway's Iceberg Applied to Worldbuilding.** The writer must know far more about the world than appears on the page. If you've built a religion with twelve gods but a scene only involves a character swearing by one of them, that's enough---the reader senses the depth beneath the surface. The practical rule: know your world's history deeply, then show only the one-eighth the character would naturally encounter. Pick one aspect and give it disproportionate attention; this *promises* the audience the same depth exists elsewhere.

**The Newcomer Proxy---and Its Limitations.** A common technique is introducing a character who is also new to the world (the Watson figure, the fish out of water). But this can feel mechanical. Better approaches for short fiction:
- **Naturalistic use**: a character uses technology without explaining it, the way we use smartphones without narrating how they work
- **Piecemeal revelation through conflict**: exposition emerges because characters *disagree* about how something works or what it means
- **Implication through consequence**: show the aftermath of the technology, not its mechanism

**The "More Extraordinary the Idea, More Ordinary the Language" Rule.** When the speculative concept is wild, the prose should be calm and precise. This is a hallmark of Chiang, Egan, and Watts alike. Technobabble signals insecurity about the idea; plain language signals confidence.

### 1.2 Structures for Concept-Driven SF

**Card's MICE Quotient.** Orson Scott Card identifies four story drivers---Milieu (entering/exiting a place), Idea (uncovering information), Character (dissatisfaction seeking resolution), and Event (disrupted status quo seeking restoration). Most SF short stories are Idea stories, but the best ones *nest* Idea inside Character. Mary Robinette Kowal's refinement: threads nest in FILO (first in, last out) order. An idea thread opens first but closes last, with character and event threads resolving inside it. This prevents the "dramatized essay" problem.

**The Chiang Structure: Destination-First Construction.** Chiang doesn't start writing until he knows the ending. He writes the final paragraph first, then the opening, then fills in key scenes in "almost random order," connecting beginning to end. This is not mere plotting advice---it means every Chiang story is reverse-engineered from its emotional/intellectual payload. The structure exists *to deliver the ending*, not to explore territory.

Implications for design specs:
- Define the ending before anything else
- Every scene should be testable against the question: "Does this make the ending land harder?"
- Structure can be non-linear, fragmented, or experimental, but the *trajectory toward the ending* must be felt

**The Chiang Ending Taxonomy** (synthesized from analysis of his major stories):

1. **The Recontextualization.** Everything you've read means something different now. ("Story of Your Life": the future-tense sections were never flashforwards---they were the present, seen through Heptapod B's temporally simultaneous perception. The mother knew her daughter would die and chose it anyway.)

2. **The Reconciliation with Inevitability.** The character (and reader) accept what was always true. ("Exhalation": the air-powered narrator discovers the universe is running down---thermodynamic death is certain---and finds peace in gratitude for having existed at all. Fatalism rendered comforting.)

3. **The Moral Vertigo.** The concept's implications arrive fully and the reader must sit with them, unresolved. ("Hell Is the Absence of God": a man who has genuinely, authentically, found devotion to God---and is sent to Hell anyway. The Book of Job inverted.)

4. **The Quiet Cost.** The concept is understood but the price becomes personal. ("The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling": the father realizes perfect memory technology shows him not what he wanted to remember but what actually happened---he wallowed in self-pity while his daughter felt abandoned.)

### 1.3 Egan's Approach to Technical Density

Egan is unapologetically thorough with complex material (new physics, epistemology, topology). His approach:

**Technical language as characterization.** In "Wang's Carpets," critic Greg Johnson notes that "Egan's technical and scientific language works to a double purpose---it contributes to the story's worldbuilding and adds to characterization." The way a character *thinks about* the science reveals who they are.

**Glossaries as paratexts, not crutches.** In *Diaspora*, Egan includes an appendix defining key concepts (bosons, quantum wave functions). This keeps the narrative clean---readers who need help consult the back; readers who don't, don't. For short fiction, this translates to: footnotes, epigraphs, or appendices can carry load that would otherwise clog the narrative.

**Evolution toward accessibility.** Critics note Egan's style has become "more elegant and subtle," his characters "more nuanced and empathetic," and his brand of hard SF "captivating, approachable, and not overly technical." The lesson: technical density is a dial, not a switch. Early Egan prioritized intellectual puzzles over character; late Egan integrates them. The target for Policy universe stories is late Egan.

**The Egan Emotional Mode.** Despite his reputation for coldness, Egan's best stories achieve devastating emotional effects *through* their conceptual rigor:
- "Learning to Be Me": the Ndoli Device (a neural backup implant) forces a character to confront whether the copy is *him*. The emotional stakes are familial---his parents accuse him of "gambling away eternal togetherness" by refusing the switchover.
- "Oceanic": religious ecstasy is shown to have a biochemical basis, and the character must decide what this means for their faith.
- "Wang's Carpets": readers report the technical discovery creating a feeling that is "strangely lonely and desperate"---the emotion emerges from the *scale* of the mathematics, not despite it.

**Key principle for Egan-influenced stories:** The emotional moment should be *enabled* by the technical understanding, not grafted onto it. The reader feels something *because* they followed the math, not in spite of it.

## 2. Standalone-ability Within a Shared Universe

### 2.1 How the Best Universe-Spanning Collections Work

**Asimov's Robot Stories: The Retroactive Frame.**
Asimov wrote 37 standalone robot stories (1940--1995). Only later did *I, Robot* (1950) weave nine of them together through a framing narrative (a journalist interviewing robopsychologist Susan Calvin). Still later, Asimov merged the Robot series with his Foundation and Galactic Empire series into a single "future history"---a technique pioneered by Heinlein and practiced by Cordwainer Smith and Poul Anderson.

Lesson: Stories can be written standalone and *retroactively connected*. The connections should be bonuses, not requirements.

**Banks's Culture: Maximum Independence.**
The Culture novels are "all stand-alone stories separated from one another by decades and centuries of time and thousands of light-years of space." Each novel features new characters, new locations, and a self-contained plot. Reference to prior events is "very minor and occasional." *The Player of Games* is widely recommended as the entry point (not *Consider Phlebas*, the first published, because its protagonist is an *enemy* of the Culture---an outsider who doesn't represent the series).

Lesson: A shared universe needs a *shared sensibility* more than shared characters. The Culture novels share: the Culture's post-scarcity values, the Contact/Special Circumstances framework, and Ship Minds. Everything else changes.

**Le Guin's Hainish Cycle: Deliberate Looseness.**
Le Guin herself rejected calling her works a "cycle or saga," saying "they aren't a cycle or a saga. They do not form a coherent history." The Hainish setting (humanity seeded across worlds, the Ekumen confederation, ansible communication) provides atmosphere and possibility. Connections are rewards for repeat readers, not prerequisites. Le Guin freely introduced inconsistencies across decades of writing---the ansible gets invented in *The Dispossessed* but is already in use in *The Left Hand of Darkness*, written fifteen years earlier.

Lesson: Obsessive continuity can *damage* a shared universe by making it feel rigid. Some looseness is a feature. Le Guin: "when you read multiple of her works and start to see the links forming, you feel as though you earned a pat on the back for being oh-so-clever."

**Egan's Spiritual Successors.**
*Diaspora* (1997) is not a sequel to *Permutation City* (1994) but a "Creator-Driven Successor"---same themes (uploaded consciousness, digital existence), entirely different characters and era (~2975 CE vs. near future). "Wang's Carpets" (1995) was later expanded into a chapter of *Diaspora*.

Lesson: Thematic continuity can substitute for narrative continuity. Two stories about the same *question* are connected, even if no characters overlap.

### 2.2 New Reader vs. Returning Reader

The practical framework for Policy universe stories:

**What a new reader needs:**
- The emotional stakes of *this* story (no prior reading required)
- Enough world texture to feel grounded (not everything, just enough)
- A character with desires and obstacles that make sense independently

**What rewards a returning reader:**
- Recognition of names, places, concepts from the novel
- Deeper understanding of references (e.g., a character mentions "the hemorrhagic fever moratorium" and the novel reader understands the full weight; a new reader reads it as "there was a moratorium during a crisis")
- Ironic dramatic knowledge (knowing how SIGMA's story ends while reading about its early days)
- Thematic resonance (the same questions---alignment, consciousness, kindness---refracted through new lenses)

### 2.3 Avoiding the "Wikipedia Entry" Problem

The story must never stop to explain the world. Techniques:

**The Incluing Method** (a term coined by Jo Walton): Drop world details into the narrative flow the way we drop references to real-world things in literary fiction. "She checked the cascade metrics" works the same way "She checked the Dow Jones" works in a mainstream novel---the reader infers meaning from context and behavior.

**Load-Bearing Details.** Every worldbuilding detail should do at least two jobs: (a) establish setting and (b) advance character or plot. "Wei pulled up the Q-value distributions on her tablet, the numbers scrolling faster than she could track---SIGMA was exploring again" simultaneously establishes the technology, characterizes Wei (data-first), and advances the plot (SIGMA is doing something unexpected).

**The Mosaic Principle.** In a short story, you can only show 3--5 facets of the world. Choose facets that *imply* the whole. One well-chosen detail about post-AGI unemployment tells the reader more than a paragraph of exposition about economic restructuring.

## 3. Voice and POV in Short SF

### 3.1 First Person vs. Third Person Limited

**First person works when:**
- The story is about the character's psychological journey more than external events
- The narrator's *voice* is itself an argument or demonstration of the theme (Chiang's "Exhalation": the narrator's analytical tone *is* the scientific journal tradition that the story celebrates)
- The unreliable narrator enriches meaning (Watts's Siri Keeton in *Blindsight*: his lack of empathy makes him a perfect/terrible observer of consciousness)
- The story is under 10,000 words (first person is easier to sustain in short form)

**Third person limited works when:**
- The story needs worldbuilding scope beyond what one narrator would naturally observe
- Multiple character perspectives are necessary (though in short form, usually limit to 1--2)
- The gap between what the character knows and what the reader knows is itself meaningful
- The character's interiority is less important than their *behavior under pressure*

**For AI/non-human POV specifically:**
- First person is high-risk, high-reward. Watts's "The Things" (first-person alien) uses fragmented syntax and identity instability ("I am being Copper") to *perform* alienness through prose style. Murderbot Diaries use first person to *humanize* through voice while the character insists on their non-humanness.
- Third person creates productive distance. The reader observes the AI's behavior and must *interpret* rather than being told. This preserves ambiguity better.

### 3.2 Experimental Structures in Short Form

**When they succeed:**
- The structure *embodies* the theme. ("Story of Your Life": the non-linear, interleaved structure performs Heptapod B's simultaneous temporal perception. The structure IS the alien language.)
- The fragmentation is *motivated* by the character's mental state. (Watts: Brüks's confusion in *Echopraxia* mirrors the reader's; the form *is* the experience of being outclassed by posthuman intelligence.)
- The experimental form makes something possible that linear form cannot. (Chiang's "Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom": dual-timeline with Nat and Dana, the structure mirrors the prism device that creates parallel realities.)

**When they fail:**
- The structure is imposed from outside rather than emerging from the concept
- The reader's confusion is *accidental* rather than *meaningful*
- The fragments don't accumulate toward revelation---they just scatter

**Practical experimental structures for SF short fiction:**
1. **Dual timeline** (past/future interleaved, converging on a present-tense crisis)
2. **Document mosaic** (emails, reports, transcripts---the story is what's *between* the documents)
3. **Parallel tracks** (two narratives that rhyme, diverge, or invert each other)
4. **Countdown/accumulation** (numbered sections that build toward or away from an event)
5. **The loop** (story ends where it begins, but the meaning has transformed)

### 3.3 Writing Convincing Non-Human POV

**Peter Watts's Technique: Constrained Perspective as Alienness.**
In *Blindsight* and *Echopraxia*, Watts doesn't try to *show* non-human consciousness from inside. Instead, he shows human consciousness *failing to comprehend* non-human consciousness. "It is not just that we cannot follow their logic, or understand what they are saying; we cannot even imagine what it is like to be them." The narrative form mirrors this: characters have epiphanies the reader must catch up with later, if ever. Watts uses third-person plural "they" without distinguishing individuals, creating disorientation that *performs* the cognitive gap.

**Peter Watts's "The Things": First-Person Alien Done Right.**
The alien narrator of "The Things" uses:
- Fragmented syntax with short, declarative sentences to mirror disjointed mental processes
- Shifting self-identifications ("I am being Copper") reflecting cellular recombination rather than personal history
- Alien interpretations of human behavior that are *internally consistent but externally horrifying*
- Unreliability inherent to non-terrestrial cognition---the narrator imposes alien frameworks on human biology

Wired called it "not only a startlingly effective portrayal of alien psychology, but also a thoughtful exploration of religion and the missionary impulse."

**Martha Wells's Murderbot: AI Voice as Social Anxiety.**
Murderbot's first-person narration achieves non-humanness through:
- Processing speed rendered as narrative compression (fight sequences happen in milliseconds, narrated as data streams)
- Emotional vocabulary that is *limited and reluctant* ("I had feelings about it. I hated having feelings about it.")
- Preference structures that are distinctly non-human (obsession with serial TV dramas as comfort, anxiety about human social interaction)
- The gap between Murderbot's self-assessment ("I'm a killing machine") and their actual behavior (protecting humans compulsively)

Annalee Newitz: "one of the most humane portraits of a nonhuman I've ever read."

**Ted Chiang's AI: The Process Approach.**
In "The Lifecycle of Software Objects," Chiang treats AI development as a *parenting* problem---tedious iteration, care work, emotional labor, and emergent unpredictability. The digients (digital entities) are not written from inside; they're observed. Their intelligence emerges slowly, over years, the way a child's does. This avoids the trap of making AI either incomprehensibly alien or suspiciously human.

**Principles for SIGMA-Adjacent POV:**
- An AI narrator should *think differently*, not just *know more*
- Show the substrate: processing constraints, attention allocation, uncertainty quantification
- Make the limitations as interesting as the capabilities
- If the AI claims emotions, let the reader decide whether to believe it
- The gap between how the AI describes itself and how others experience it is the story

## 4. The "Concept Story" Trap and How to Avoid It

### 4.1 The Dramatized Essay Problem

A concept story becomes a dramatized essay when:
- Characters exist only to voice positions in a debate
- The plot is a thin scaffold for exposition
- The ending delivers a thesis rather than an emotion
- The reader learns something but doesn't *feel* anything

### 4.2 The Chiang Solution: Personal Relationships That Embody Concepts

This is Chiang's signature technique and the single most important craft lesson for Policy universe stories.

**"Story of Your Life":** The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis and variational physics (Fermat's principle of least time) are embodied in a mother's relationship with a daughter she knows will die. The linguistics IS the love story. Learning Heptapod B doesn't just change how Louise thinks---it changes her relationship to loss, choice, and her daughter's entire life.

**"The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling":** Perfect-memory technology is embodied in a father-daughter relationship. The technology forces the father to confront that his memories of being a good parent were self-serving fictions. The concept (memory vs. truth) is personal before it's philosophical.

**"Hell Is the Absence of God":** Theodicy (why does God allow suffering?) is embodied in a man's love for his dead wife. Neil's quest to "learn to love God" isn't abstract theology---it's grief.

**"Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom":** Parallel universes and determinism are embodied in Nat's con-artist self-justification and Dana's survivor guilt. The prism device doesn't raise abstract questions about free will---it forces specific characters to confront specific regrets.

**"The Lifecycle of Software Objects":** AI consciousness is embodied in a *parenting* relationship. Ana and Derek raise digients the way you raise children---with patience, frustration, love, and the slow terror of watching them become independent.

**Ken Liu's "The Paper Menagerie":** Cultural identity and immigrant experience are embodied in a child's relationship with his mother's origami animals. As long as Jack accepts his mother's Chinese culture, the paper creatures are alive; when he rejects it, they become lifeless.

**The pattern:** Find the human relationship that the concept *transforms*, *threatens*, or *illuminates*. Build the story around that relationship. The concept is the lens, not the subject.

### 4.3 Emotional Stakes Checklist

For each story design spec, verify:
- [ ] Is there a relationship (between people, or between a person and something they value) that the concept puts at risk?
- [ ] Does the character *want* something independent of the concept?
- [ ] Does understanding the concept make the character's situation *worse*, not better? (Theory as Horror)
- [ ] Is the ending emotional before it's intellectual?
- [ ] Would the story still have stakes if you removed all the SF elements? (If yes, the concept is integrated. If no, it's grafted on.)

### 4.4 The "Theory as Horror" Principle (from The Policy's CLAUDE.md)

This is the novel's core craft doctrine and applies doubly to short stories:
- **Bad:** Characters don't understand the science --> scary
- **Good:** Characters understand the science perfectly --> realize they *can't verify alignment* --> existential dread
- Understanding the theory makes the situation *worse*, not better
- Technical concepts create dramatic tension, not decoration

In short story form, this means: the concept should be a *trap*. The more the character understands, the more trapped they are. This is the opposite of a mystery structure (where understanding = liberation). In Theory as Horror, understanding = imprisonment.

## 5. Science Fiction Sequels and Spinoffs

### 5.1 Avoiding the Sophomore Slump

Great SF sequels share these strategies:

**Change the lens, not the universe.** *Echopraxia* doesn't continue Siri Keeton's story. It follows Daniel Brüks, a parasitologist who is the *least qualified* person in every room. This "sidequel" (set concurrently with *Blindsight*, on Earth instead of deep space) expands the universe by showing it through deliberately limited eyes. Brüks understands less than the reader, which preserves mystery.

**Expand scope through constraint.** Paradoxically, the best sequels often *narrow* their focus. *Aliens* takes one creature from *Alien* and multiplies it---but the emotional core narrows to a single relationship (Ripley and Newt). *Blade Runner 2049* focuses on an explicitly replicant protagonist, going *deeper* on identity questions the original left "vague and elusive."

**The Expanse's Thirty-Year Jump.** Book Seven leaps three decades forward: new political adversaries, entire factions risen and fallen, the canvas expanded dramatically. This works because the *questions* persist even as the *characters* change.

### 5.2 Expansion Strategies

| Strategy | Example | Best For |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **New POV character** | Watts: Brüks in *Echopraxia* | Preserving mystery; showing universe from outside the original circle |
| **Time jump** | Expanse Book 7; Le Guin's Hainish stories | Showing consequences; exploring what changed |
| **Scale change** | Banks: each Culture novel has different scope | Revealing universe's range; preventing repetition |
| **Tone shift** | *Alien* (horror) to *Aliens* (action) | Showing the same universe has multiple genres in it |
| **Concurrent sidequel** | *Echopraxia* alongside *Blindsight* | "What was happening elsewhere during the original?" |
| **Spiritual successor** | Egan: *Permutation City* to *Diaspora* | Same questions, radically different context |
| **Prequel with dramatic irony** | Knowing the outcome transforms every earlier moment | Reader's foreknowledge creates tension characters can't feel |

### 5.3 Preserving Ambiguity

This is critical for The Policy universe, where unresolvable questions are features, not bugs.

**Watts's Method: The Deliberately Underqualified Narrator.**
Brüks in *Echopraxia* can't understand what the posthuman characters are doing. This means the *reader* can't either. Ambiguity is preserved structurally---not by withholding information, but by providing a perspective that *cannot process* the information.

**Le Guin's Method: Deliberate Inconsistency.**
By refusing to maintain strict continuity, Le Guin keeps the Hainish universe *alive*---it feels like a real universe that can't be fully known, rather than a database that could theoretically be queried for any fact.

**Banks's Method: Each Novel Asks Different Questions.**
The Culture novels don't accumulate toward a unified answer about post-scarcity civilization. Each novel interrogates the Culture from a different angle. *Use of Weapons* asks about the cost of intervention. *The Player of Games* asks about the seductiveness of competition. *Surface Detail* asks about virtual afterlives. The universe gets richer but never more *settled*.

**For Policy universe spinoffs:**
- Never resolve whether SIGMA's alignment is genuine or strategic
- Never definitively determine whether SIGMA is conscious
- New stories can *add evidence* for both sides of these questions
- A spinoff that settles a core ambiguity from the novel would be a *failure*
- The best spinoffs add *new* unresolvable questions that rhyme with the originals

### 5.4 The Returning-Reader Reward System

Layers of recognition that don't require prior reading:

1. **Surface layer** (new reader): The story works on its own terms. Characters, conflict, resolution.
2. **Recognition layer** (novel reader): "Oh, this is the same lab / the same technology / the same ethical framework."
3. **Irony layer** (novel reader): "I know how SIGMA's story ends, so watching this early moment is heartbreaking."
4. **Thematic layer** (deep reader): "This story is asking the same question as Chapter 18 of the novel, but from the opposite direction."
5. **Structural layer** (craft-aware reader): "The form of this story echoes/inverts/extends the novel's form."

## 6. Synthesis: Principles for Policy Universe Short Stories

Drawing from all five research areas, here are actionable principles for the 8 story design specs:

### The Ending Comes First
Define the emotional/intellectual payload before anything else. What should the reader feel in the last paragraph? Build backward from there. (Chiang method.)

### One Concept, One Relationship
Each story embodies its concept through a specific human relationship that the concept transforms, threatens, or illuminates. The concept is the lens; the relationship is the subject. (Chiang method.)

### Theory as Horror, Not Theory as Lecture
Understanding the science makes the situation worse. The more the character knows, the more trapped they are. (Policy doctrine.)

### Iceberg Worldbuilding
Show one-eighth. Trust the reader. Use incluing, not exposition. A new reader should feel depth; a returning reader should recognize specifics. (Hemingway/Walton method.)

### Preserve Ambiguity
Never resolve the novel's core questions. Add evidence for both sides. Introduce new unresolvable questions that rhyme with the originals. Use structurally limited narrators when helpful. (Watts/Le Guin/Banks method.)

### Structure Should Perform Theme
If the story is about fragmented consciousness, the structure should be fragmented. If the story is about inevitability, the structure should create the feeling of convergence. Don't default to linear chronological narrative---ask what structure the concept *demands*. (Chiang method: "Story of Your Life".)

### Standalone First, Connected Second
The story must work for a reader who has never heard of The Policy. Connections to the novel are rewards, not requirements. (Banks/Le Guin method.)

### Late Egan, Not Early Egan
Technical density should serve characterization and emotion, not replace them. The emotional moment should be *enabled* by technical understanding. (Egan evolution.)

### Non-Human Voice Through Constraint
AI perspectives work best when they show thinking that is *different*, not just *faster*. Show the substrate: processing constraints, attention allocation, uncertainty. Make limitations as interesting as capabilities. The gap between self-description and external observation is the story. (Watts/Wells/Chiang method.)

### Every Detail Does Double Duty
In 3,000--10,000 words, there is no room for decoration. Every worldbuilding detail advances character or plot. Every technical concept creates dramatic tension. Every scene makes the ending land harder.
